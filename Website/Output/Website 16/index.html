Principles

  • Restraint over decoration. If a layout starts looking like a rainbow, we've gone too far.
  • One job per colour. Violet means "click me". Lime means "look here". Nothing else competes.
  • Space is a material. Off-white covers half of any layout on purpose.
FoundationsLogo

Logo

The cleartax wordmark is the primary and preferred expression of the brand. It is always set as a single, unbroken word in lowercase, in the custom brand typeface, and is used in full wherever space allows.
The lowercase is deliberate: it signals approachability and clarity rather than institutional formality, a company that removes complexity rather than adds to it. Never capitalise the wordmark to “Cleartax” or “ClearTax” in logo form, and never letterspace, condense, or re-set it in another typeface. The wordmark is artwork, not text. Always place the supplied file; never retype it.

Download logo files↓

Lockups

Two lockups cover every case — Ledger White on dark, Vault Navy on light. There is no third variant.

Construction

The wordmark is a single, fixed artwork. Never retype, resize individual letters, adjust spacing, or rebuild it. Always use the master artwork at its original 218 × 50 unit ratio of 4.36:1.
The wordmark is one continuous word, “clear” and “tax” are never separated, hyphenated, stacked, or differentiated by colour or weight. The join is the idea: clarity and tax as a single thing. Its character is geometric and rounded, even stroke weight, open counters, soft terminals, which is what gives it warmth without losing authority. Proportions, kerning and stroke weight are fixed and must never be altered. Do not add a symbol, icon, tagline, or descriptor into the wordmark itself; endorsements and product names sit outside the logo in an approved lockup.

Locked ratio

218 : 50 — 4.36 : 1. Scale the whole mark, never a part of it.

x-height

33.7 units — 69% of the 48.5-unit ascender on the “l”, which gives the mark its low, wide stance.

Overshoot

The rounded letters c, e, and both a’s slightly extend above and below the baseline by 0.65 units. This is intentional and keeps the rounded letters visually balanced with the straight letters.

The “t”

The “t” deliberately stops short of the “l”, so the word reads as one shape rather than two peaks.

Logo clearspace

Clear space is measured using x, the height of the c. Keep at least 1x of empty space on all four sides of the logo. Nothing should enter this area, including text, lines, other logos, or image edges. When possible, allow even more space.

To ensure optimal readability, there should always be a minimum amount of clear space around the cleartax logo. This space isolates the mark from any competing graphic elements, such as other logos or body copy, that may conflict with, crowd, or diminish the overall impact of our mark.

Note: there is no separate small-size cut of the wordmark. Where it would land below the minimums shown here, do not shrink it further — leave it out and let the layout carry the brand instead.

FoundationsColor

Color

Our palette is built the way our platform is built: every element has exactly one job, and none of them compete. Most compliance software looks like the anxiety it manages dense, blue-grey, defensive. Ours doesn't. We use a deep, near-black navy to hold authority, a warm off-white to create room to breathe, an electric blue to show the way forward, and a single charged lime to mark the one thing that matters most on any given screen. Read in order, the four colours describe the life of a transaction: it is recorded (Ledger White), it is secured (Vault Navy), it is actioned (Nova Blue), and where necessary, it is flagged (Flux Lime). The palette is not decoration applied to the product. It is the product, expressed as colour.

The idea behind the palette

Ledger White · #F2F1F0

Ledger White is the surface everything else is written on. It is deliberately not pure white: a warm, paper-soft off-white that reads as considered rather than clinical, premium rather than sterile. Pure white is a screen default. This is a choice.

It carries the most fundamental idea in our brand: that clarity is created by what you remove. In a category where every competitor crowds the frame with data, our willingness to leave space is itself a statement of confidence. Only a company certain of its answer can afford to give it room.

Its job: the ground. Backgrounds, canvases, cards, negative space, and the calm between elements. It should feel like the layout is breathing.

Never: used as a text or accent colour, or replaced with pure #FFFFFF in brand applications.

Vault Navy · #05022A

Vault Navy is where the brand's authority lives. Almost black, but not black, there's a violet depth in it that keeps it from feeling cold or corporate-generic. It is the colour of substance, permanence and safekeeping: the vault, the record, the thing that does not move.

This is the colour of our promise. When a CFO hands us the data that determines their legal standing, Vault Navy is the visual form of the assurance we give back. It carries seriousness without severity, the tone of an institution but a modern one.

Its job: all primary typography, the wordmark, headers, and dark-mode surfaces. Where the brand needs to feel solid and unmoved, this is the colour.

Never: lightened into a mid-grey-blue, or used for interactive elements - Vault Navy states, Nova Blue acts.

Nova Blue · #5446FF

Nova Blue is the only colour in the palette with velocity in it. Electric, saturated, unmistakably digital - this is intelligence and technology made visible, the signal that something here is alive and can be acted on.

It is our direction giver. Against the stillness of Ledger White and the weight of Vault Navy, Nova Blue is the one thing moving, and that contrast is exactly why it works: on a calm surface, a single charged blue element becomes impossible to miss. It carries our engineering, the automation, the AI, the ten thousand invoices a second without ever having to say so.

Its job: primary CTAs, links, active and selected states, interactive controls, data highlights and illustration accents. If it's blue, it does something.

Never: used as a background for long-form text, or applied so widely that it stops meaning “act here.”

Flux Lime · #C8F006

Flux Lime is the sharpest instrument in the palette and the most disciplined. Charged, unexpected and unapologetically energetic, it is the colour of change in motion - the moment a threshold is crossed, a saving is found, a risk surfaces, a mandate goes live.

It exists because compliance is not a flat landscape. Somewhere in every screen, one number, one alert, one opportunity outranks everything else. Flux Lime is how we say so without shouting. It is also, quietly, our signal of innovation and optimism and proof that a compliance brand can have a pulse. Its power comes entirely from its scarcity. Used once, it is a spotlight. Used twice, it is noise.

Its job: the single most important element in a view. Key metrics, critical alerts, a hero highlight, a moment of celebration.
The rule: one instance per screen. If a designer needs two, one of them isn't important.

Never: as body text on white or light backgrounds, or as a large background field.

Colour Usage Ratio

Ledger White: 60% Vault Navy 25% Nova Blue 10% Flux Lime 5%

Four colours. Four jobs. Every layout is a hierarchy, and this is it.

Ledger White holds sixty percent of the frame, space is the majority shareholder. Vault Navy takes twenty-five, carrying the brand's weight and every word of substance. Nova Blue takes ten, marking the paths forward. Flux Lime takes five or less, and only ever once. This ratio is not a stylistic suggestion. It is the mechanism that makes the palette work at all. Nova Blue and Flux Lime sit close to complementary on the colour wheel, at equal volume they fight, and the layout becomes loud and cheap. Kept to fifteen percent combined, they become precision instruments against a calm ground. Discipline in the palette is how the brand demonstrates discipline in the product. A crowded layout is a broken promise.

Colour Best Practices

Four colours, Hold the ratio, stay inside the approved pairings, and never introduce a fifth.

Do

  • Let Ledger White hold roughly 60% of the layout - the empty space is the design
  • Four colours, four jobs. Navy carries the brand, off-white holds the space, lime highlights, violet converts. Every colour has exactly one role — that's what keeps layouts calm
  • Do keep colour usage consistent across components

Don't

  • Don't put colour on colour - Flux Lime on Nova Blue, or any pair of the four
  • Don't introduce a fifth brand colour, or tint Vault Navy to invent a new one
  • Don't spend Flux Lime twice on a screen - imagine you only have permission to use it once or twice in an entire URL
  • Don't set body copy in Flux Lime, and keep Flux Lime plus Nova Blue together under 15%
  • Don't use Flux Lime for large areas of dense content

Typography

The guide below is the shorthand for setting type across Cleartax communications. Two families, one hierarchy: they never cross. A heading is never set in Google Sans, and body copy is never set in Inter Tight.

Download typography files↓

What we needed
the type to do

Our typography has to work across the website, campaigns, sales decks, whitepapers, event assets and documents, in the hands of marketers, agencies and partners across every market we operate in. It has to hold a headline on a six metre backdrop and a footnote in a sixty page PDF.

It also has to solve a specific problem. Every tax and compliance platform we compete with is set in some variation of a corporate grotesque, and the whole category reads as cold and slightly defensive. We wanted a system with authority in it, without inheriting that chill.

Why two families and not one: Inter Tight alone would have worked. It has a text cut, it is highly legible, and one family is simpler to manage. We rejected it because Inter is a neutral grotesque all the way through, and a brand built entirely on it lands exactly where the category already is. We would have looked correct and forgettable.

Google Sans alone would also have worked, and it fails in the other direction. Its geometric roundness is friendly at paragraph size and goes soft at headline size, where a claim needs to feel settled and immovable.

So we split the roles. Inter Tight where we assert. Google Sans where we explain. One family would have been easier to run. Two is the only way to get authority and approachability into the same page without one cancelling the other.

The families

Aa

Primary typeface — Inter Tight

Display, H1–H6. Headlines, hero lines, campaign lines, section titles, cover slides and large display figures.

Inter Tight is the close spaced cut of Inter. The tight setting is the reason we chose it. Set large, the words pull into a single block instead of drifting apart into a string of letters, so the line holds as one shape. It also keeps long terms on one line. “Continuous compliance, without borders” and “Input tax credit reconciliation” are lines we have to set in banners and on covers, and a wider face breaks them across two.

The letterforms carry no stylistic quirks. No unusual terminals, no distinctive g, nothing that dates. Our headlines are usually the entire idea, and a typeface with an accent competes with them.

It is a variable font from thin to black, so one file gives us the full hierarchy and the site stays light.

Aa

Secondary typeface — Google Sans

Body copy, paragraphs, labels, overlines, captions and long form documents.

The main reason is the wordmark. The cleartax logotype is drawn from a geometric skeleton with circular forms, soft terminals and an even stroke weight, and Google Sans is built from the same vocabulary. Put the logo above a paragraph and they read as related. Set the same paragraph in Inter and they read as two decisions made by two people. This is the one argument in this section you can verify by looking.

The second reason is temperature. Round bowls, open counters and soft terminals make it noticeably warmer than a grotesque at paragraph size, and that is where people spend real time with us: whitepapers, guides, mandate explainers, case studies. It is also what keeps the overall system from tipping cold, since Vault Navy, tight headlines and disciplined layouts are all pulling the other way.

Google Sans Text, the reading cut, is tuned for small sizes, and the family covers the scripts we need as we move into more markets.

123

Numbers

Our proof is numerical, so figures get set with intent.

Hero statistics go in Inter Tight. A number that large is doing the job of a headline, and the tight setting keeps the digits together as one shape.

Anything in a column uses tabular figures, through the OpenType tnum feature. Both families support them. Proportional figures shift digits between rows, which makes a comparison table or a pricing grid hard to scan. Tabular figures line every decimal point down the page.

Running prose uses proportional figures, where even spacing reads better and nothing needs to align.

Chosen to last

Both are open licensed under the SIL Open Font License, so there is nothing to buy, renew or clear when we hand assets to an agency or partner in a new market.

Both sit in the Google ecosystem, so when someone opens Slides or Docs to build something at short notice, the brand fonts are already in the menu. Brand systems break at the point of friction, and this removes it.

Add both to the organisation's Workspace font list so they load by default rather than sitting behind the “more fonts” menu, and build the Slides and Docs templates on them. If product adopts the system later it extends cleanly, since both faces were drawn for interfaces in the first place.

Typography in use

  • Headlines are always in sentence case - capitalise only the first word and any proper noun
  • Section headers, subheads and CTAs follow the same rule of sentence case
  • Display and heading styles carry −0.5px tracking; the type is tight by design.
  • Paragraph and label styles tracking sit at 0.
  • Overline is the only style that opens up, at tracking at +1px, and the only one set in uppercase.
  • Font weights never reach past Bold.
  • Always avoid widows and orphans.

Iconography guidelines

Consistency

Keep the entire icon set visually unified. Every icon should feel like it belongs to the same family, regardless of its subject or complexity.

Construction

Build icons on a consistent square grid with predictable proportions. Use the same base geometry, alignment, corner treatment, and spacing rules across the set.

Stroke

Use a consistent stroke weight across icons. Keep strokes uniform and avoid mixing thin and heavy outlines within the same icon.

Corners & endings

Use rounded corners and rounded line caps to maintain the friendly, modern character of the system. Apply the same treatment consistently across all icons.

Colour

Icons are two-tone by construction: Vault Navy 950 draws the structure and Nova Blue 500 marks the single detail that names the icon, on the neutral chip. Never a third colour, a gradient, or a tint outside these.

Meaning

Icons should communicate their meaning immediately. Prefer familiar, standard symbols over abstract metaphors or decorative interpretations.

Optical balance

Icons should be visually balanced rather than mathematically identical. Adjust proportions, spacing, and visual weight so icons appear consistent when placed together.

Scalability

Design icons to remain clear at small sizes. Simplify unnecessary detail when an icon needs to work at smaller scales, while preserving its recognisable shape.

Sizing

Use the predefined icon sizes — 32, 24 and 20 — across products and interfaces. Do not arbitrarily resize individual icons or distort their proportions.

Why an outline set

At 1px on a 32px box, an outline icon carries about the same optical weight as the stem of body text — so it reads as part of the label, not as a button. In a compliance product the eye should land on the number, not the picture beside it.

Why the chip

The 32px circle makes an icon a self-contained object: it drops into a table row, card, or category chip with its padding already built in, so nobody decides the spacing twice. It also gives the two-tone drawing a neutral ground on any surface.

Best practices

Do

  • Use the approved icon library wherever an existing icon covers the required meaning
  • Maintain the same stroke weight, corner radius, and line-cap treatment across the set
  • Align icons to the defined grid and preserve their intended proportions
  • Scale the complete icon proportionally rather than stretching individual parts
  • Choose icons based on their literal and functional meaning
  • Maintain consistent visual weight between icons placed in the same component or view
  • Simplify complex icons when required for smaller sizes
  • Reuse the same icon wherever the same meaning appears across the product
  • Use the approved icon sizes and spacing defined by the design system
  • Create new icons using the established construction rules when no suitable icon exists

Don't

  • Mix icons from different libraries or visual styles within the same interface
  • Change the stroke weight of an existing icon
  • Stretch, compress, rotate, or mirror icons — directional icons may mirror in RTL locales, nothing else
  • Redraw an existing icon to create a slightly different version
  • Introduce unapproved colours, gradients, shadows, or effects
  • Add unnecessary decorative details to functional icons
  • Use an abstract icon when a familiar symbol communicates more clearly
  • Place icons inconsistently within their bounding boxes
  • Scale icons disproportionately to fit a component
  • Use detailed icons at sizes where their forms become unclear or visually noisy

Spacing guidelines

Image and text spacing

Images are visually heavier than text. Adding extra space creates balance between vibrant imagery and content, making it easier to read — and more space creates better contrast between a headline and tinted text.

Component spacing

Use less space within components and more space between them. Smaller gaps bind related elements into one unit; larger gaps separate one unit from the next. The gap is the grouping.

Spacing best practices

Do

  • Use values from the approved spacing scale consistently
  • Use smaller spacing within components and larger spacing between sections
  • Increase spacing for larger hierarchy jumps — between sections and major content groups
  • Use spacing to create clear relationships between related elements
  • Maintain consistent spacing between similar components throughout the interface
  • Use repetition of spacing values to create rhythm and visual consistency
  • Use spacing to improve readability rather than relying only on typography changes

Don't

  • Invent custom spacing values just to make an element fit
  • Use inconsistent gaps between similar elements or components
  • Give unrelated elements the same spacing when their hierarchy is different
  • Use excessive spacing that breaks the relationship between related content
  • Use typography changes to fix spacing problems
  • Adjust individual margins arbitrarily when the scale already provides a value
  • Let spacing vary unnecessarily across responsive breakpoints

FoundationsLayout grids

Layout grids

A responsive grid system adapts to screen size and orientation, ensures consistency and hierarchy across layouts.

Columns, gutters, and margins

The responsive layout grid is made up of three elements: columns, gutters, and margins.

Columns

Most content is placed in the areas of the screen that contain columns. The number of columns displayed in the grid is determined by the breakpoint range, a range of predetermined screen sizes. A breakpoint can correspond with mobile, tablet, or other screen types.

Gutters

A gutter is the space between columns that helps separate content.

Gutter widths are fixed values at each breakpoint range. To better adapt to a given screen size, gutter widths can change at different breakpoints. Wider gutters are more appropriate for larger screens, as they create more open space between columns.

Margins

Margins are the space between content and the left and right edges of the screen. Margin widths are defined using fixed or scaling values at each breakpoint range. To better adapt to the screen, the margin width can change at different breakpoints. Wider margins are more appropriate for larger screens, as they create more whitespace around the content.

Library of layout grid options with various columns and sizes that fits any type of content perfectly in your web, tablet, and mobile screens.

Layout grid best practices

Do

  • Align content to the grid — use columns and gutters as consistent alignment points for text, images, and components
  • Preserve the same grid logic within each breakpoint; columns, gutters, and margins change only by the defined responsive rules
  • Use the grid to establish hierarchy — wider spans for primary content, narrower spans for secondary
  • Allow elements to span multiple columns when needed — intentional spans, not one column forced on everything
  • Maintain consistent horizontal alignment — related sections share starting and ending points where possible
  • Design for the smallest supported viewport first, so content reflows naturally as the grid expands

Don't

  • Align elements by eye when a grid alignment point is available
  • Create custom gutters or margins for individual components without a clear layout requirement
  • Mix different grid systems on the same breakpoint — spacing and alignment start to feel inconsistent
  • Force content to fill every column — empty grid space can be intentional and helps hierarchy
  • Let components break the grid without purpose — full-bleed is an intentional exception, not an accident
  • Use the grid as a decorative element — grid lines are a structural guide, not visual styling

Under 150ms reads as instant feedback (hover snaps, the cursor). 200–300ms suits a full view or panel change. Closing is always faster than opening — leaving should feel quicker than arriving.

Easing

power2.out
Used on every entrance — view fade-in, nav accordion open, lightbox open. Fast start, gentle settle.
power1.in
Used only on the lightbox close — starts slow, accelerates away, signalling "leaving."
power3.out
Used for cursor tracking only — the snappiest curve, reserved for something that follows the pointer every frame.

When to animate

Do

  • Confirm a view or state change (route switch, accordion, lightbox)
  • Soften an abrupt layout shift instead of a hard cut
  • Stagger a short list of blocks (12 or fewer) so it reads as one arrival

Don't

  • Animate on every hover — reserve it for clickable elements
  • Stagger a long list; past ~12 items it reads as lag, not polish
  • Chain more than two motions for one action

Every transition here is skipped outright under prefers-reduced-motion — views still switch, the accordion still opens, just without the tween.

Focus & interaction for developers

Make sure every interactive element has a visible 2px Generic Black focus outline when users navigate with a keyboard. Never remove or hide the focus state. On this site that is a :focus-visible outline, never suppressed; the custom cursor is decorative only and is skipped on touch, coarse pointers, and under prefers-reduced-motion, so no interaction ever depends on it.

Alt text for developers

Use an empty alt="" for icon images and provide the icon name through the button’s aria-label and hover/focus tooltip — so screen readers announce the name once instead of reading the same information twice. For icons still named “Group…” in Figma, use their Figma ID as a temporary accessible name until they receive a proper one. Never leave an interactive icon without an accessible name.

Accessibility best practices

Do

  • Maintain sufficient colour contrast between text, icons, and their backgrounds
  • Use Vault Navy for dense body copy where higher readability is required
  • Provide a visible focus state for every interactive element
  • Use descriptive labels for interactive icons and controls so their purpose is clear to screen readers (for developers)
  • Provide meaningful alt text for informative images and illustrations (for developers)
  • Use empty alt text (alt="") for purely decorative imagery (for developers)
  • Ensure information is not communicated through colour alone
  • Keep text readable at different sizes and zoom levels
  • Respect reduced-motion preferences; never make interactions depend on animation (for developers)

Don't

  • Use low-contrast colour combinations for important text or UI elements
  • Use Nova Blue for small body text where it fails the required contrast ratio
  • Remove or hide focus states from keyboard-accessible elements
  • Rely on colour alone to communicate errors, status, selection, or success
  • Use decorative icons as the only way to communicate an important action or status
  • Give informative images meaningless or generic alt text (for developers)
  • Duplicate information by having screen readers read the same visible label and image description twice (for developers)
  • Make custom cursors or hover effects essential for understanding or operating an interface
  • Use excessive motion without respecting prefers-reduced-motion
  • Sacrifice readability for visual styling when choosing brand colours
GuidelinesVoice & content

Voice & content

This page's own copy is the spec — short, declarative sentences that state a rule and then the reason for it. Every doc-lead on this site follows the same shape; here's why, made explicit.

Principles

  • State the rule, then the reason. "Off-white covers half of any layout on purpose" — not "we recommend generous use of white space for a clean aesthetic."
  • One sentence, one idea. If a lead paragraph needs a semicolon to fit everything in, it's two sentences that haven't been split yet.
  • Name the next step, not the mechanism. Buttons say "Book a demo" and "Talk to an expert" — never "Submit" or "Learn more about our platform."
  • Flag gaps instead of hiding them. See Design tokens → Known gaps — say what's missing and why, rather than smoothing it over.

In practice

Avoid

"Oops! Something went wrong. Please try again later."

Prefer

"The portal didn't accept this return. Retry now, or save it as a draft and file before the 20th."

Avoid

"Our platform leverages automation to seamlessly revolutionize your filing experience."

Prefer

"Restraint over decoration. If a layout starts looking like a rainbow, we've gone too far." — the Overview page, unchanged.
